# Evidence Base for an Eight-Block Body-Recomposition Coaching System

This brief treats Schoenfeld’s hypertrophy framework, the *Renaissance Diet 2.0* priority hierarchy, and McDonald’s stubborn-fat model as the conceptual starting point, then updates the actionable rules with evidence available through September 2026. The most important update is that **the fundamentals survived better than many of the fine-grained rules**: progressive resistance training, enough weekly hard sets, adequate protein, control of energy balance, and adherence matter greatly; exact frequency, nutrient timing, training to absolute failure, “fat-burning zones,” and specialized stubborn-fat tactics matter much less. The 2026 ACSM resistance-training position stand synthesized 137 systematic reviews involving more than 30,000 participants and similarly concluded that relatively few prescription variables meaningfully alter the primary adaptation once progressive resistance training is in place. citeturn19view0 **Confidence: high.**

**Confidence convention used below:** **High** = supported by multiple systematic reviews/meta-analyses, major consensus/position statements, or unusually consistent controlled evidence; **Medium** = reasonable controlled evidence but meaningful uncertainty, indirectness, or heterogeneity; **Low** = useful coaching heuristic, limited direct trials, or an extrapolation rather than a scientifically established cutoff.

## A. Decision tree: RECOMP vs CUT vs LEAN GAIN

The first correction to common coaching practice is that **body-fat percentage should not be treated as a hard biological switch**. Field estimates differ across methods, and even DXA requires standardized positioning, feeding, hydration, and scanning conditions to reliably detect small longitudinal changes. Therefore, an estimated “18%” versus “20%” should never by itself decide the phase. citeturn18search1turn18search0 **Nana et al., 2015; Tornero-Aguilera et al., 2022. Confidence: high.**

The practical phase decision should instead combine estimated adiposity, training status, recent progress, primary goal, dieting history, and tolerance for gaining or losing scale weight. There are no randomized trials validating the exact male/female body-fat cutoffs below; they are **routing heuristics**, not medical categories. **Confidence: low for exact thresholds; medium for the underlying decision logic**, based on energy-restriction and surplus studies. citeturn14view4turn21view2turn14view5

| Starting situation | Default route | Expected trajectory | Evidence / confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Clearly above preferred adiposity**; practical routing band roughly **>20% male / >30% female**, or waist/fat loss is clearly the dominant goal | **CUT**, performed like a “muscle-preserving recomp”: moderate deficit + progressive RT + high protein | Default target about **0.5–0.75% body weight/week**; slower as the person becomes leaner | A trial in elite athletes favored ~0.7%/week over faster loss for lean-mass and strength outcomes. Garthe et al., 2011. citeturn21view2 **Confidence: medium** |
| **Middle adiposity band**, roughly **12–20% male / 20–30% female**, and novice, detrained, returning after layoffs, or still progressing rapidly | **RECOMP** | Scale weight approximately stable or drifting slowly downward while waist decreases and performance rises | High-protein energy restriction plus training can produce simultaneous fat loss and lean-mass gain, although dramatic examples come from unusually intensive short studies. Longland et al., 2016. citeturn14view4 **Confidence: medium** |
| Same middle band, but **advanced and clearly plateaued**, with fat loss as the dominant goal | **CUT** | Prefer slow loss, especially near the lean end | Recomposition becomes harder to detect as remaining adaptive potential falls; exact transition point has not been established experimentally. Garthe et al., 2011; Slater et al., 2019. citeturn21view2turn6search1 **Confidence: medium-low** |
| Same middle band, advanced, already satisfied with fat level, and muscle/strength is clearly the priority | **LEAN GAIN** | Very slow positive weight trend | A larger surplus does not reliably buy proportionally more hypertrophy and can buy more fat. Helms et al., 2023. citeturn14view5 **Confidence: medium** |
| **Lean**, practical routing band roughly **≤12–15% male / ≤20–23% female**, with no compelling reason to get leaner | **LEAN GAIN** or maintenance-strength phase | Slow gain; do not force rapid scale gain | Energy surplus requirements for maximal hypertrophy remain poorly quantified; small surpluses are the lower-risk default. Slater et al., 2019; Helms et al., 2023. citeturn6search1turn14view5 **Confidence: medium-low** |
| Lean and explicitly pursuing further fat loss for a sport/physique endpoint | **CUT**, but conservatively | Usually **~0.25–0.5%/week**, with closer recovery monitoring | The cost of aggressive restriction tends to rise as preserving lean tissue and performance becomes more important; physique-athlete literature supports slower rates. Helms et al., 2014; IOC, 2023. citeturn17search0turn19view7 **Confidence: medium** |

**Training age should be defined by adaptation, not simply calendar years.** For the agent, “novice” means meaningful load/repetition progression is still possible most weeks; “intermediate” means progress is more often measured across several weeks; “advanced” means adding muscle or strength requires longer blocks and small improvements. Exact year-based definitions are arbitrary. **Confidence: low as taxonomy, high that response varies substantially with training status.** citeturn14view0turn19view0

**Recomposition should be expected, not promised.** It is most plausible when one or more favorable conditions exist: new or renewed resistance training, substantial remaining training potential, moderate/higher fat stores, an improved protein intake, or a previous program that provided inadequate stimulus. Longland et al. demonstrated lean-mass gain despite an approximately 40% energy deficit in young men consuming 2.4 g/kg/day of protein with very intensive training, but this was a four-week proof-of-principle experiment and is **not justification for prescribing a 40% deficit**. citeturn14view4 **Longland et al., 2016. Confidence: high that recomp is physiologically possible; low that those magnitudes generalize.**

**Expected phase rates for the operating system:**

| Phase | Coach default | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| RECOMP | roughly **−0.25% to ~0% BW/week** | **Coach heuristic, low confidence.** Weight is deliberately a weak KPI; waist, photos and training progression carry more weight. Evidence supports possible simultaneous changes but not this exact optimal band. citeturn14view4turn21view2 |
| CUT | **−0.5 to −0.75% BW/week** | **Medium confidence.** Lean/advanced trainees use the slower side; people with substantially more fat may tolerate the faster side. Garthe et al., 2011. citeturn21view2 |
| LEAN GAIN | approximately **+0.1 to +0.25% BW/week**, generally slower as training age rises | **Low-medium confidence.** The exact optimum is unknown; this is deliberately conservative because aggressive surpluses appear to raise fat gain more reliably than muscle gain. Helms et al., 2023. citeturn14view5 |

**Safety override:** suspected eating disorder, pregnancy/lactation, unexplained rapid weight change, recurrent stress injuries, significant menstrual disruption, persistent libido/endocrine-type symptoms, syncope/presyncope, or signs suggestive of problematic low energy availability move the case out of automated cutting and into **ASK HUMAN / clinician** territory. The agent should never diagnose RED-S from symptoms or invent hormone/lab values; IOC guidance treats diagnosis and risk stratification as a clinical task. citeturn19view7turn18search10 **IOC, 2023. Confidence: high.**

## B. Training for hypertrophy + strength

The most important post-Schoenfeld update is that the evidence increasingly favors a **dose-response-with-diminishing-returns model**, rather than one universal optimal set number. Pelland et al.’s 2026 meta-regression included 67 studies and 2,058 participants: increasing weekly volume predicted more hypertrophy and strength, but with diminishing returns; frequency independently showed a clearer dose-response for strength than hypertrophy. citeturn14view0 **Pelland et al., 2026. Confidence: high.**

**MEV and MRV therefore should be treated as operational concepts, not measured physiological constants.** No high-quality literature establishes that a novice has “X MRV” while an advanced trainee has exactly “Y MRV.” A better definition is: **MEV proxy = lowest current dose at which the trainee demonstrates repeatable progress; MRV proxy = the point beyond which added work stops improving adaptation and begins degrading performance/recovery.** The scientific evidence supports volume dose-response and diminishing returns, not fixed universal landmarks. citeturn14view0turn15search27turn19view0 **Pelland et al., 2026; Baz-Valle et al., 2022; ACSM, 2026. Confidence: high for concept, low for fixed numbers.**

**Practical volume landmarks**

| Training status | Conservative starting “MEV proxy” | Common productive range to explore | “MRV warning zone,” not a hard ceiling |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Novice / returner | ~4–8 direct-equivalent sets/muscle/week | ~6–12 | Once approaching ~12–16, require evidence that more volume is actually helping |
| Intermediate | ~6–10 | ~8–16 | ~16–20 should trigger closer fatigue/performance review |
| Advanced | ~8–12 | ~10–18 | ~18–22+ only when the individual is clearly benefiting and recovering |

These are **coach starting landmarks, not validated cutoffs**. A systematic review found roughly 12–20 weekly sets favorable for quadriceps and biceps in the populations studied, while the newer ACSM overview concluded that ≥10 weekly sets tends to enhance hypertrophy; Pelland’s newer meta-regression indicates continued average benefit with diminishing returns rather than a sharp ceiling. citeturn15search27turn19view0turn14view0 **Baz-Valle et al., 2022; ACSM, 2026; Pelland et al., 2026. Confidence: medium for broad productive ranges, low for experience-specific cut points.**

Count a direct set as roughly one set and an indirect set as a partial contribution rather than pretending a bench-press set gives chest and triceps an identical stimulus. Pelland et al. found the strongest statistical fit when indirect sets were fractionally counted. citeturn14view0 **Pelland et al., 2026. Confidence: medium-high.**

**Frequency.** Two exposures per muscle per week is an excellent default, but **not because “twice weekly” has a unique anabolic property**. The older 2016 meta-analysis favored ≥2× over 1×, whereas subsequent volume-equated work and the 2026 dose-response analysis make frequency look more like a way to distribute volume and preserve set quality; hypertrophy effects independent of volume can be small or negligible. citeturn18search7turn0search25turn14view0 **Schoenfeld et al., 2016; Grgic et al., 2019; Pelland et al., 2026. Confidence: high.**

Operationally, use **2×/muscle/week by default; 3× when weekly volume, strength-practice needs, session length, or per-session fatigue justify it.** Full-body versus split routines produce similar adaptations when relevant training variables are comparable. citeturn15search31turn14view0 **Ramos-Campo et al., 2024; Pelland et al., 2026. Confidence: high for equivalence, medium for 2–3× default.**

**Loading spectrum.** Muscle hypertrophy can be generated across a broad range of loads when sets are sufficiently effortful, whereas heavier loading is consistently superior for improving high-load one-repetition-maximum strength. Meta-analysis found similar hypertrophy with lower and higher loads but an advantage of higher loads for 1RM/isometric strength. citeturn15search14turn15search18 **Schoenfeld et al., 2017; Refalo et al., 2021. Confidence: high.**

Practical allocation:

| Purpose | Default repetition zone | Confidence |
|---|---:|---|
| Primary strength-biased compound work | ~3–6 or 4–8 reps | **High** that heavier work improves 1RM-specific strength; ACSM 2026 highlights ≥80% 1RM. citeturn19view0 |
| Mixed hypertrophy/strength work | ~6–12 | **Medium-high** as a practical efficiency range, not a uniquely hypertrophic range. citeturn15search18 |
| Stable machine/isolation work | ~8–20, occasionally higher | **High** that moderate/light loads can hypertrophy when sufficiently hard; **medium** for this exact practical range. citeturn15search14turn15search18 |
| Very light 20–30+ rep work | Optional, not required | **Medium.** Can work, but discomfort and the need to approach failure make it less efficient for many trainees. citeturn15search18 |

**Proximity to failure.** Absolute muscular failure is **not required** for hypertrophy. Meta-analytic evidence does not show a consistent advantage of failure over non-failure training, while newer dose-response work suggests hypertrophy tends to improve as sets finish closer to failure. The useful synthesis is therefore “close enough to failure, not failure at all costs.” citeturn3search24turn0search23 **Grgic/Refalo et al., 2022–2024. Confidence: high.**

A practical default is **1–3 RIR on most hypertrophy working sets**, allowing occasional 0–1 RIR on safe, stable isolation/machine work. Heavy compounds can often sit at **2–4 RIR** when accumulating quality strength work because repeated failure adds fatigue without a demonstrated requirement for greater adaptation. Exact RIR prescriptions are coaching approximations; RIR itself also has measurement error. citeturn0search3turn0search23turn19view0 **Refalo et al., 2023; Robinson et al., 2024; ACSM, 2026. Confidence: medium-high.**

**Exercise selection.** Keep enough exercise stability to measure progression, but select movements that collectively train the target musculature through useful joint functions and lengths. Strategic exercise variation can alter regional hypertrophy, but random exercise rotation makes overload and response harder to assess. citeturn3search26turn19view0 **Kassiano et al., 2022; ACSM, 2026. Confidence: medium.**

Full range of motion remains the safest default for most movements, especially because it simultaneously trains strength through the complete task. Longer-muscle-length training has accumulated supportive evidence and may produce additional hypertrophy in some contexts; however, current evidence does **not** justify replacing every full-ROM exercise with extreme lengthened partials. citeturn11search1turn15search1turn15search9 **Schoenfeld & Grgic, 2020; Wolf et al., 2025; Wolf et al., 2025. Confidence: medium-high for full ROM; medium for superiority of long-length strategies.**

**Rest intervals.** A 2024 Bayesian meta-analysis suggests a small hypertrophic advantage for rest periods longer than 60 seconds, plausibly because longer rests preserve repetitions/load. In practice, **2–3+ minutes for demanding compounds and ~1–2+ minutes for smaller isolation work** is a sound starting point; extend rest whenever performance is being limited by breathing rather than target-muscle output. citeturn15search0turn15search8 **Singer et al., 2024; Grgic et al., 2018. Confidence: medium-high.**

**Tempo.** There is no evidence-based need to deliberately slow every repetition. A systematic review found similar hypertrophy over a broad roughly 0.5–8-second repetition-duration range, with extremely slow repetitions appearing less favorable. Use controlled technique, a purposeful eccentric, and strong concentric intent. citeturn15search15 **Schoenfeld et al., 2015. Confidence: medium.**

**Concurrent cardio.** Aerobic work can coexist with hypertrophy and strength training. Whole-muscle hypertrophy and maximal strength are generally preserved when programs are sensibly designed, though some meta-analyses detect small interference effects for lower-body/fiber-level adaptations, particularly with running or very demanding concurrent workloads. citeturn15search2turn15search6turn15search17 **Schumann et al., 2022; Lundberg et al., 2022; Huiberts et al., 2024. Confidence: high that moderate concurrent training is compatible; medium for modality-specific interference.**

**Progression algorithm**

1. Keep the same key exercises long enough to evaluate them. Select a rep range and RIR target. **Coach heuristic; confidence: medium**, supported by progressive-RT evidence. citeturn19view0
2. First add repetitions while staying inside the prescribed RIR; once all work sets reach the top of the rep range at target RIR, add the smallest practical load increment. **Coach heuristic; confidence: medium.** citeturn19view0
3. Do **not** add volume simply because a new week started. Increase a muscle by roughly 1–2 sets/week only when progress or stimulus appears inadequate **and** performance, soreness, joints, sleep, and motivation indicate recovery capacity. The 1–2-set increment is a practical control rule, not an RCT-derived optimum. citeturn14view0 **Confidence: low-medium.**
4. If performance is progressing, soreness is manageable, and technique is stable, hold volume. More work has diminishing returns. citeturn14view0 **Pelland et al., 2026. Confidence: high.**
5. If performance falls across multiple exposures while effort feels higher and recovery is worsening, first remove fatigue rather than automatically adding stimulus. **Coach heuristic grounded in load-management logic; confidence: medium-low.**

**Deloads should be reactive or strategically scheduled, not treated as mandatory anabolic events.** A 2024 trial found that a one-week complete resistance-training cessation in the middle of a nine-week program produced similar hypertrophy but worse lower-body strength outcomes than training continuously. Survey data show strength/physique athletes commonly deload, but surveys do not prove efficacy. citeturn15search3turn15search7 **Coleman et al., 2024; Rogerson et al., 2024. Confidence: medium.**

A coach deload can therefore mean **about 5–7 days with ~30–50% fewer hard sets, more RIR, and optionally a modest load reduction rather than total cessation**. Those percentages are practical heuristics, not validated optimal doses. citeturn15search3 **Confidence: low for exact prescription; medium that fatigue reduction is preferable to obligatory complete rest.**

## C. Nutrition for simultaneous fat loss + muscle retention/gain

The Renaissance Diet hierarchy largely survives contact with newer evidence **as an operational priority order**, not as a mathematically validated ranking: energy balance establishes the direction of mass change; adequate protein materially affects lean-mass adaptation; carbohydrate/fat distribution should support performance, health and preference; meal timing produces smaller conditional effects; food quality mainly supports nutrient adequacy, satiety and adherence; supplements sit downstream of all of these. Protein and energy evidence is considerably stronger than evidence that fine nutrient timing independently changes long-term hypertrophy. citeturn5search8turn6search18turn19view5 **Morton et al., 2018; ISSN, 2017; Henselmans et al., 2022. Confidence: high for calories/protein priority, medium for treating the entire hierarchy as an effect-size ranking.**

**Energy targets**

| Phase | Starting target | Evidence / confidence |
|---|---|---|
| RECOMP, novice/returner or moderate-high adiposity | Around maintenance to **~5–15% below estimated maintenance** | Exact range is a **coach heuristic, low-medium confidence**. Recomp has occurred under much larger deficits experimentally, but that should not be interpreted as optimal. Longland et al., 2016. citeturn14view4 |
| CUT | Usually **~10–25% below maintenance**, but steer by observed weight-loss rate rather than the calculated percentage | **Medium confidence.** A 19% intake reduction produced slower, more favorable loss than a 30% reduction in Garthe’s small athlete trial. citeturn21view2 |
| LEAN GAIN | Start around **+3–8% above observed maintenance** | Exact optimum remains unknown; **low-medium confidence**. An eight-week trial comparing maintenance, ~5%, and ~15% surpluses in trained lifters supports avoiding large surpluses because faster weight gain preferentially increased skinfolds without clearly buying proportionally more muscle/strength. citeturn14view5 |

**Maintenance is best discovered empirically.** Formula-derived maintenance calories are useful only as an initial estimate; the operating system should calibrate them from approximately two weeks of intake, morning body-weight trend, and activity under reasonably consistent conditions. This is a **coach heuristic** rather than evidence that exactly 14 days is optimal. **Confidence: medium-low.**

**Protein.** A strong default for resistance-training adults is **1.6–2.2 g/kg/day**. Morton et al.’s meta-analysis estimated the average breakpoint for additional fat-free-mass benefit near 1.6 g/kg/day, with considerable uncertainty above that level; higher intakes can be useful insurance during energy restriction. citeturn5search8turn17search25 **Morton et al., 2018; Jäger et al., 2017. Confidence: high.**

During a meaningful cut, especially in a lean trainee, **~1.8–2.4 g/kg/day** is a reasonable practical range. Longland’s 2.4 g/kg group gained more lean mass and lost more fat than a 1.2 g/kg group during a severe deficit plus intensive training, while physique-athlete literature proposes even higher values when expressed relative to fat-free mass in very lean, heavily dieting athletes. citeturn14view4turn17search0 **Longland et al., 2016; Helms et al., 2014. Confidence: medium-high.**

For someone with high adiposity, using total body weight can produce unnecessarily large protein numbers; using estimated lean mass or a reasonable target body weight is a practical workaround. The exact conversion rule is not validated. **Confidence: low as a dosing method**, while the need for adequate protein itself is high-confidence. citeturn5search8

**Protein distribution.** Splitting protein over roughly **3–5 meaningful servings**, often around **0.3–0.5 g/kg/meal**, is sensible, while total daily intake remains the primary target. A meal near training is reasonable, but there is no narrow “anabolic window” requiring immediate protein consumption. citeturn17search25turn6search18turn17search8 **Jäger et al., 2017; ISSN nutrient-timing position stand, 2017; Roberts et al., 2020. Confidence: medium-high.**

**Carbohydrate.** Carbohydrate should largely be allocated according to total calories, preference, and training demands rather than a belief that high carbohydrate intake independently causes hypertrophy. A 2026 meta-analysis of 11 studies found no significant hypertrophy advantage from higher carbohydrate intake (pooled SMD 0.15, p=0.23), and certainty was graded low. citeturn19view4 **Henselmans et al., 2026. Confidence: medium that carbohydrate itself is not strongly anabolic; low certainty around the exact effect.**

For resistance performance, the 2022 systematic review found carbohydrate was unlikely to improve a **fed-state** session of up to roughly ten sets per muscle group, but benefits become more plausible with glycogen depletion, twice-daily training, fasting, or unusually high-volume sessions. citeturn19view5 **Henselmans et al., 2022. Confidence: medium-high.**

Thus the agent should preserve carbohydrate preferentially when a trainee reports falling training quality during a cut, but should not tell a low-volume trainee that massive carbohydrate intake is necessary for hypertrophy. citeturn19view5turn19view4 **Confidence: medium.**

**Dietary fat.** A broad practical range around **0.6–1.0 g/kg/day** works well for many body-recomposition diets, with the remainder of calories allocated mainly to carbohydrate. Physique-athlete reviews have proposed wider ranges such as 0.5–1.5 g/kg/day, but direct experimental evidence identifying an optimal fat intake for hypertrophy is weak. citeturn17search3turn17search7 **Iraki et al., 2019. Confidence: low-medium for the exact range.**

**Macro templates — operational defaults**

| Phase | Protein | Fat | Carbohydrate |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| RECOMP | ~2.0 g/kg | ~0.8 g/kg | Remaining calories |
| CUT | ~2.0–2.4 g/kg | ~0.6–0.8 g/kg | Remaining calories, protecting training performance |
| LEAN GAIN | ~1.6–2.0 g/kg | ~0.8–1.0 g/kg | Remaining calories |

Protein ranges are well-supported; fat and exact phase-specific allocation are more heuristic. citeturn5search8turn14view4turn17search3 **Morton et al., 2018; Longland et al., 2016; Iraki et al., 2019. Confidence: high for protein, low-medium for exact fat allocation.**

**Illustrative 80-kg trainee with a measured/estimated maintenance of 2,600 kcal/day**

| Goal | Calories | Protein | Fat | Carbs |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| RECOMP, −10% | 2,340 | 160 g | 64 g | ~281 g |
| CUT, −20% | 2,080 | 176 g | 56 g | ~218 g |
| LEAN GAIN, +5% | 2,730 | 144 g | 72 g | ~377 g |

These are arithmetic examples, **not universal prescriptions**. Their purpose is to show that protein and a practical fat allocation can be set first and carbohydrate allowed to float with available energy. The evidence does not require these exact ratios. citeturn19view4turn5search8 **Confidence: high for arithmetic, low for universal applicability.**

**Guía flexible de comidas para pegar en el agente**

**Prioridad:** energía semanal → proteína diaria → distribución de carbohidratos/grasas según entrenamiento y preferencia → horario → detalles menores. La proteína y la energía tienen evidencia más fuerte que el “timing” fino. citeturn5search8turn6search18 **Morton et al., 2018; ISSN, 2017. Confianza: alta.**

Para cada comida principal, construir primero una porción que contribuya de forma significativa a la meta diaria de proteína; después añadir frutas/verduras y una fuente de carbohidrato o grasa compatible con el presupuesto energético. No existe obligación de usar alimentos “fitness” específicos. **Heurística de coaching; confianza: media**, consistente con recomendaciones de nutrición para atletas de fuerza y physique. citeturn17search8turn17search3 **Roberts et al., 2020; Iraki et al., 2019.**

Antes del entrenamiento, usar una comida tolerable con proteína y, cuando ayude al rendimiento, carbohidrato; después del entrenamiento, volver simplemente a la pauta normal de proteína y energía. No exigir una ventana de minutos. citeturn6search18turn19view5 **ISSN, 2017; Henselmans et al., 2022. Confianza: media-alta.**

En una dieta con hambre alta, mover más calorías hacia comidas voluminosas y alimentos que el usuario encuentre saciantes; en una fase de ganancia con dificultad para comer suficiente, hacer lo contrario. La respuesta de hambre es individual y la implementación concreta es una **heurística de adherencia**, no una fórmula fisiológica universal. **Confianza: media-baja.**

Do not allow a “perfect” meal plan to defeat adherence. A less elegant food pattern that consistently hits appropriate energy and protein targets is preferable to a theoretically optimized plan the client cannot sustain; this is a coaching inference from the stronger effects of energy/protein and the comparatively small evidence for timing minutiae. citeturn5search8turn19view4turn6search18 **Confidence: medium.**

## D. Stubborn-fat layer: what survives, what does not

McDonald’s strongest enduring idea is that **adipose tissue is not metabolically identical across body regions**. Upper- and lower-body depots differ in blood flow and responsiveness to lipolytic stimuli, and sex/depot differences in adipose physiology are well documented. citeturn10search1turn10search17turn10search13 **Jensen et al., 1997 and subsequent regional physiology work. Confidence: high that regional biology differs.**

What should **not** survive as a coaching conclusion is the leap from “regional lipolysis differs” to “therefore a specialized exercise sequence can reliably strip fat from a chosen region.” Acute mobilization of fatty acids and chronic loss of adipose tissue are not the same outcome. citeturn10search29turn14view6 **Stallknecht et al., 2007; Ramirez-Campillo et al., 2022. Confidence: high.**

| Claim from the stubborn-fat model | Current reading | Evidence grade |
|---|---|---|
| Some regions mobilize stored fat less readily than others | **Still holds mechanistically.** Regional differences in adipose blood flow and lipolysis are real. citeturn10search1turn10search17 | **Moderate-high** |
| Adrenergic receptor distribution contributes to regional differences | **Plausible and biologically real, but commonly oversimplified.** Receptor behavior depends on physiological context; a 2008 local alpha-2 blockade experiment did not show the simple expected control of lipolysis under high circulating adrenaline. citeturn10search0 | **Medium** |
| Increasing local muscle activity can acutely increase nearby blood flow/lipolysis | **Can occur acutely.** This does not establish meaningful chronic spot reduction. citeturn10search29turn14view6 | **Medium-high mechanistically; low for practical fat loss** |
| Targeted resistance/abdominal work reliably burns adjacent fat | **Mostly falsified.** A meta-analysis of 37 comparisons produced pooled ES −0.03 (95% CI −0.10 to 0.05), essentially no spot-reduction effect. citeturn14view6 | **High** |
| Fasted cardio is required for stubborn fat | **Not supported for long-term body composition.** It may alter acute substrate oxidation but has not demonstrated a dependable body-fat advantage over fed exercise when overall intake is considered. citeturn10search15turn10search19 | **Medium-high** |
| A single newer trial can show regional effects under unusual conditions | A 2023 abdominal-exercise study reported local fat reduction, creating a legitimate conflict, but one small trial does not overturn the broader null meta-analysis. citeturn10search10turn14view6 | **Low-medium; unresolved replication issue** |

The spot-reduction meta-analysis is unusually useful for effect-size honesty: the pooled effect of **−0.03** is not merely “statistically nonsignificant”; it is centered essentially at zero. citeturn14view6 **Ramirez-Campillo et al., 2022. Confidence: high.**

Therefore, **“stubborn fat” should be an observation about sequence, not a separate diagnosis or special protocol.** A client may lose visible fat rapidly from the face, arms or upper trunk while the lower abdomen/hips/thighs change later; that does not imply that energy balance has stopped working or that the client needs a special drug, supplement, fasted state, or local exercise protocol. Regional physiology is real, but the intervention evidence still points back toward continued whole-body fat loss. citeturn14view6turn10search1 **Confidence: high for rejecting spot reduction; medium for predictions about individual regional sequence.**

**Evidence-backed non-drug levers**

A sustained energy deficit remains the principal lever for reducing total fat mass. Resistance training protects or improves muscular adaptation; cardio can increase activity/energy expenditure; none has been shown to selectively target a “stubborn” depot. citeturn14view4turn14view6turn10search36 **Longland et al., 2016; Ramirez-Campillo et al., 2022; 2024 aerobic-exercise dose-response meta-analysis. Confidence: high.**

Use **walking or other low-fatigue aerobic work first** when the primary purpose is to increase expenditure without compromising resistance-training performance. Cycling can also be attractive when lower-body fatigue needs controlling; concurrent-training research suggests running may create somewhat more interference in some contexts, although the magnitude depends on workload and outcome. citeturn15search6turn15search17 **Lundberg et al., 2022; Huiberts et al., 2024. Confidence: medium.**

Use HIIT when the trainee enjoys it, needs aerobic performance, or values time efficiency—not because HIIT has unique access to stubborn fat. **Confidence: high that there is no established depot-specific advantage; medium on the choice of modality.** citeturn14view6turn15search2

Maintain a **step/activity floor** during dieting because spontaneous daily movement can otherwise vary and obscure the relationship between food intake and the observed weight trend. The exact number of steps is individual; the agent should anchor the person to their established baseline rather than issue a universal 10,000-step law. **Coach heuristic; confidence: medium-low.**

Sleep is relevant because severe experimental sleep restriction lowers muscle protein synthesis, making poor sleep particularly undesirable during a calorie deficit even though these short-term mechanistic studies do not quantify the effect on months-long hypertrophy. citeturn21view4turn21view5 **Saner et al., 2020; Lamon et al., 2021. Confidence: medium.**

**Coach heuristics, explicitly not evidence claims:** when the client says “only my lower belly/hips are left,” do **not** change the plan solely because the remaining fat is regional; first confirm that the multiweek weight/waist trend is still moving. Do not add fatigue to “attack” a body part. Only modify calories/activity if whole-body progress has actually stalled. **Confidence: low as exact algorithm, high in being consistent with the absence of meaningful spot reduction.** citeturn14view6

**Not for agent prescription.** The stubborn-fat literature and McDonald’s book discuss adrenergic pharmacology/stimulant concepts because catecholamine receptors participate in adipose-tissue lipolysis. That material can be described as physiology, but pharmacological manipulation of those pathways is **not a coaching-agent intervention**; no dose, combination, cycle, or “stack” should be generated. Human receptor-blockade studies illustrate mechanism, not a self-treatment protocol. citeturn10search0 **Simonsen et al., 2008. Confidence: high for the boundary; medium for translating receptor experiments to real-world fat loss.**

## E. Recovery & adherence

Recovery should not be turned into a mystical score. The agent needs enough variables to detect whether the trainee can **repeat high-quality training, sustain nutrition, and avoid accumulating signs of excessive restriction**. Direct evidence that any single consumer recovery metric predicts hypertrophy is weak; performance trend, sleep, soreness/joint symptoms, appetite and willingness to train are more useful as a practical composite. **Confidence: medium-low for the composite; high that recovery constraints modify training tolerance.** citeturn19view0turn15search7

**Sleep.** Experimental sleep deprivation provides stronger mechanistic evidence than the common coaching slogan “sleep equals gains.” Five nights with only four hours in bed reduced myofibrillar protein-synthesis rates compared with normal sleep in young men; another crossover experiment found one night of total sleep deprivation reduced post-meal muscle protein synthesis by about **18%**. These are acute mechanistic effects and must not be translated into “X% less muscle growth” over a training block. citeturn21view4turn21view5 **Saner et al., 2020; Lamon et al., 2021. Confidence: high for acute effect, low for a precise chronic hypertrophy penalty.**

Operationally, protect a regular sleep opportunity and treat recurrent sleep restriction as a reason **not to escalate volume or dieting aggression**. The exact number of hours required for maximal hypertrophy has not been established. **Coach inference; confidence: medium.** citeturn21view4turn21view5

**Stress.** The agent should record unusually high life stress because it can change adherence, appetite, sleep, subjective fatigue and willingness to train. It should not manufacture cortisol explanations or infer endocrine dysfunction from mood. IOC RED-S literature specifically emphasizes interactions among energy availability, mental health, fatigue and psychological conflict, reinforcing the need for a human gate when symptoms become clinically concerning. citeturn18search10turn19view7 **IOC subgroup reviews, 2023. Confidence: medium-high.**

**Hunger management should begin with the least complicated intervention:** check whether the rate of weight loss is unnecessarily fast; ensure protein is adequate; move food toward times of day when hunger is worst; favor foods/meals the trainee personally finds filling; and remove unnecessary dietary rigidity. The exact satiety hierarchy varies between individuals. **Coach heuristic; confidence: medium**, supported indirectly by energy/protein and diet-break evidence. citeturn14view4turn14view7

**Diet breaks are adherence tools, not metabolism “resets.”** In the ICECAP trial, resistance-trained adults completing intermittent restriction with one-week energy-balance breaks achieved similar fat loss and fat-free-mass retention to continuous restriction; the intermittent group did report less hunger/desire to eat and greater satisfaction. citeturn14view7 **Peos et al., 2021. Confidence: high that breaks are not inherently superior for fat loss; medium that they can help appetite/adherence.**

This conflicts somewhat with smaller trials suggesting benefits for fat-free-mass retention in specific settings, which means the responsible conclusion is **“diet breaks are optional, potentially useful, but not required.”** citeturn9search1turn14view7 **Campbell et al., 2020; Peos et al., 2021. Confidence: medium.**

A one-week diet break will often raise scale weight because energy balance, carbohydrate intake and stored substrate/water have changed; this should not automatically be interpreted as rapid fat regain. The agent should resume trend analysis after normal conditions return rather than “compensating” with an aggressive deficit. **Coach inference; confidence: medium-low**, consistent with the one-week ICECAP diet-break observations. citeturn9search2 **Peos et al., 2021.**

**Mini-cuts** are best regarded as a periodization heuristic for a gaining phase that has accumulated more fat than desired, not a special metabolic intervention. Direct evidence establishing optimal mini-cut duration or deficit is lacking. **Confidence: low.**

**When to pause or stop a cut**

| Trigger | Agent action | Evidence / confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Goal is reached and further leanness is not needed | Transition to maintenance rather than inventing a reason to keep cutting | **Coach rule; medium confidence** |
| Loss persistently exceeds roughly 1% BW/week in a relatively lean resistance-trained person | Reduce deficit unless explicitly medically supervised | Faster loss was inferior to slower loss in the athlete RCT. Garthe et al., 2011. citeturn21view2 **Medium** |
| Strength/repetition performance is falling across several sessions plus sleep/hunger/recovery is worsening | First reduce dieting/training stress rather than “push harder” | **Coach inference; medium-low**, supported by recovery and energy-restriction evidence. citeturn21view2turn21view4 |
| New menstrual dysfunction, recurrent bone/stress injury, marked fatigue, concerning mood/eating behavior, or other possible RED-S symptoms | **STOP automated deficit escalation → ASK HUMAN/clinician** | IOC RED-S consensus. citeturn19view7 **High** |
| Binge/restrict cycles or eating-disorder signs appear | Do not prescribe tighter tracking or a more aggressive deficit; escalate | IOC mental-health/RED-S literature. citeturn18search10 **High** |
| User requests hormones, prescription drugs, “fat burners,” diagnostic labs, or interpretation of abnormal medical symptoms | **ASK HUMAN/qualified clinician; do not invent values or prescribe** | Safety boundary; **high confidence** |

## F. Tracking KPIs and weekly adjustment rules

The operating system should separate **signal from noise**. A single scale reading is low-quality evidence because body mass changes with gastrointestinal content, glycogen/water, sodium, menstrual-cycle-related fluid shifts and other short-term variables; repeated standardized measurements provide a better trend. Body-composition methods likewise require standardization to detect small changes. citeturn18search1 **Nana et al., 2015. Confidence: high.**

**Primary KPI stack**

| KPI | Protocol | How the agent uses it | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Body weight | Ideally morning, after bathroom, before food/drink, same scale; analyze a **7-day mean or regression trend** rather than individual days | Energy-balance direction | Self-weighing is useful for weight management; exact seven-day algorithm is a coach convention. citeturn13search23 **Medium-high** |
| Waist | Same landmark, posture, time/conditions, preferably weekly | Fat-loss corroboration when scale is ambiguous | Standardization is essential; exact weekly frequency is heuristic. **Medium** citeturn18search1 |
| Other girths | Optional: hip, thigh, chest, arm; same anatomical landmarks | Secondary body-composition signal | **Low-medium** because changes combine muscle, fat, fluid and measurement error |
| Training log | Exercise, load, reps, sets, estimated RIR; note pain/technique problems | Main muscular-performance KPI | Progressive RT is central to adaptation. citeturn19view0 **High** |
| Adherence | Calories/meal-plan adherence, protein, planned sessions, activity target | Determines whether a plateau is a programming problem or an execution problem | **Coach metric; medium-low** |
| Photos | Front/side/back, same distance, lighting, camera height, pose and preferably time of day; every ~2–4 weeks | Qualitative morphology check | **Coach heuristic; low** |
| Recovery | Sleep trend, soreness/joints, motivation, unusual fatigue, hunger | Constraint on progression | citeturn21view4turn15search7 **Medium** |

Do **not** use consumer-scale body-fat percentage as a weekly decision variable. Different measurement techniques can disagree substantially, and small changes can be inside measurement noise. Use the same method under standardized conditions only as a slow-moving secondary signal. citeturn18search0turn18search1 **Tornero-Aguilera et al., 2022; Nana et al., 2015. Confidence: high.**

**Weekly adjustment engine**

For a **CUT**, if 2–3 weeks of valid trend data show loss inside the planned band and gym performance is broadly stable, make **no adjustment**. The exact 2–3-week waiting period is a coach heuristic designed to avoid reacting to water noise. **Confidence: medium-low.** citeturn18search1turn21view2

If loss is faster than intended and performance/recovery/hunger are deteriorating, add energy or reduce expenditure. If loss is slower than intended, first verify adherence and activity before lowering calories. **Confidence: medium** for the sequence; exact size of the correction is heuristic. citeturn21view2

A practical correction is **~5–10% of current calories** or a modest increase in routine activity, followed by another observation period. Do not simultaneously slash calories and add large amounts of cardio because the system then loses causal clarity and creates unnecessary fatigue. The 5–10% figure is **not an empirically established optimum**. **Confidence: low as exact rule, medium as control-system logic.**

For **RECOMP**, do not require scale loss. Hold the plan when weight is broadly stable, waist/photos are improving, and training performance is progressing. If weight and waist are both rising persistently, reduce energy slightly; if weight is falling quickly and performance is declining, increase energy. **Confidence: low-medium as algorithm, medium that recomposition can occur without large body-mass changes.** citeturn14view4

For **LEAN GAIN**, do not respond to one flat week by increasing food. If the multiweek body-weight trend is flat **and** performance/body measurements are also stagnant, add a small amount of energy. If weight is rising well above the target rate while waist/skinfolds rise rapidly without proportionate performance progress, reduce the surplus. citeturn14view5 **Helms et al., 2023. Confidence: medium for avoiding aggressive surplus; low for exact adjustment size.**

**Adherence gate:** do not diagnose a physiological plateau when adherence data are poor. The operating system should first distinguish “the prescribed dose failed” from “the prescribed dose was not consistently delivered.” This is a control-system heuristic rather than a trial-derived threshold. **Confidence: high conceptually, low for any chosen adherence percentage.**

A useful agent threshold is **≥85–90% adherence before making fine caloric/programming changes**. This number is deliberately operational rather than scientific; it should not be presented as a biologically meaningful cutoff. **Confidence: low.**

## G. Open questions and evidence gaps

**The optimal energy deficit for true recomp in already-trained, relatively lean adults remains unknown.** We have strong proof that fat loss and lean-mass gain can coexist under selected conditions, but much of the most impressive experimental evidence comes from young participants, short durations, aggressive training protocols, or people with meaningful adaptive runway. citeturn14view4turn21view2 **Longland et al., 2016; Garthe et al., 2011. Confidence: high that the gap exists.**

**The optimal surplus for maximal muscle gain has not been established.** Evidence is much weaker than common bodybuilding precision implies. The small 2023 trial is useful because it argues against indiscriminately large surpluses, but it had only 17 completers and eight weeks of follow-up. citeturn14view5 **Helms et al., 2023. Confidence: high that uncertainty remains.**

**There is no validated universal MEV or MRV table by experience level.** Current volume evidence supports a positive dose-response with diminishing returns and large inter-individual differences; it does not identify a reliable biological “MRV = 20 sets” boundary. citeturn14view0turn15search27 **Pelland et al., 2026; Baz-Valle et al., 2022. Confidence: high.**

**The exact proximity-to-failure optimum remains unresolved.** The emerging evidence favors sufficiently close-to-failure training for hypertrophy but does not show that every set should reach 0 RIR, and RIR estimates themselves are imperfect. citeturn0search23turn0search3 **Robinson et al., 2024; Refalo et al., 2023. Confidence: high that failure is unnecessary; medium on the exact optimum.**

**Long-muscle-length training looks promising but is still being refined.** Some studies show superior regional hypertrophy versus short-length training, while newer direct comparisons suggest full ROM and lengthened partials can be more similar than early enthusiasm implied. The agent should use this as an exercise-selection refinement, not a new training religion. citeturn15search1turn15search9turn15search20 **2023–2025 evidence. Confidence: medium.**

**Carbohydrate’s independent hypertrophy effect remains uncertain.** The newest meta-analysis found no significant advantage, but GRADE certainty was low because the evidence base remains small and imperfectly controlled. citeturn19view4 **Henselmans et al., 2026. Confidence: low-medium.**

**Diet breaks probably help some people behaviorally without producing unique metabolic fat-loss effects.** The best controlled evidence in resistance-trained adults found similar fat loss and lean-mass retention to continuous dieting but better appetite-related outcomes. citeturn14view7 **Peos et al., 2021. Confidence: medium-high.**

**Spot reduction has one important modern anomaly.** The 2022 meta-analysis is strongly null overall, while a small 2023 abdominal-training trial reported a localized effect. Until independently replicated, coaching policy should follow the much larger aggregate evidence rather than promote local-fat protocols. citeturn14view6turn10search10 **Confidence: high for current policy; medium that the final scientific question is fully closed.**

**Acute sleep studies cannot tell us exactly how much hypertrophy a chronically sleep-deprived trainee loses.** They demonstrate biologically meaningful impairment in protein synthesis, but converting an 18% acute MPS reduction into “18% fewer gains” would be scientifically invalid. citeturn21view4turn21view5 **Saner et al., 2020; Lamon et al., 2021. Confidence: high.**

**Body-fat routing thresholds are not evidence-based treatment cutoffs.** They are useful only as coarse coaching context because body-composition estimation itself contains error and no RCT assigns trainees to cut/recomp/gain based on a specific BF threshold. citeturn18search0turn18search1 **Confidence: high that the limitation exists.**

**Sex-specific programming remains underdeveloped in the literature.** There is not enough evidence to automatically rewrite resistance-training programming around menstrual-cycle phase for every woman, and sex-specific concurrent-training findings remain incomplete. The agent should respond to the individual’s symptoms/performance rather than manufacture cycle-based rules. citeturn15search17 **Huiberts et al., 2024. Confidence: medium.**

**Energy availability and RED-S are clinically important but difficult to estimate accurately from consumer tracking.** The agent should therefore recognize warning signs and escalate rather than calculate a pseudo-precise diagnosis or invent endocrine markers. citeturn19view7turn18search4 **IOC, 2023; Jeukendrup et al., 2024. Confidence: high.**

## H. Agent operating rules

The eight blocks should operate as one closed-loop system:

**Bloque de evaluación → entrenamiento → nutrición → comidas flexibles → progresión → tracker semanal → recuperación → motor de decisiones.** The system changes only the minimum variable necessary, waits long enough to observe a meaningful trend, and never creates medical facts that were not supplied. This architecture is a coaching synthesis of the evidence above rather than a tested intervention in itself. **Confianza: media como sistema, alta para sus principios centrales.** citeturn19view0turn14view0turn5search8

**Reglas IF/THEN para el agente**

1. **SI** hay embarazo/lactancia, trastorno de alimentación conocido o sospechado, pérdida de peso inexplicable, síncope, lesión por estrés recurrente, alteración menstrual relevante, síntomas médicos importantes o posible RED-S, **ENTONCES** no intensifiques dieta ni entrenamiento y marca **ASK HUMAN / profesional sanitario**. No diagnostiques. citeturn19view7turn18search10 **IOC, 2023. Confianza: alta.**

2. **SI** faltan peso, talla, objetivo, historial de entrenamiento, actividad o una estimación razonable del estado corporal, **ENTONCES** recopila esos datos antes de calcular macros o volumen. **Heurística de sistema. Confianza: alta operativamente.**

3. **SI** el % de grasa es una estimación visual/BIA/báscula doméstica, **ENTONCES** trátalo como una banda aproximada y nunca como un número diagnóstico exacto. citeturn18search0turn18search1 **2022/2015. Confianza: alta.**

4. **SI** la adiposidad es relativamente alta y perder grasa es prioridad, **ENTONCES** selecciona CUT moderado antes que LEAN GAIN; conserva entrenamiento de fuerza y proteína alta. Los umbrales concretos de BF son heurísticos. citeturn14view4turn21view2 **2016/2011. Confianza: media.**

5. **SI** el usuario es novato, vuelve de una pausa o todavía progresa rápidamente y quiere mejorar composición corporal, **ENTONCES** RECOMP es una opción razonable, especialmente si no necesita ganar peso rápidamente. citeturn14view4 **Longland et al., 2016. Confianza: media.**

6. **SI** el usuario es avanzado, relativamente magro y su prioridad principal es ganar músculo, **ENTONCES** usa LEAN GAIN con superávit pequeño en vez de “bulk” agresivo. citeturn14view5 **Helms et al., 2023. Confianza: media.**

7. **SI** estás en CUT, **ENTONCES** apunta normalmente a ~0.5–0.75% del peso/semana; reduce la velocidad conforme el atleta sea más magro/avanzado o si el rendimiento se deteriora. citeturn21view2 **Garthe et al., 2011. Confianza: media.**

8. **SI** el usuario pregunta por una RECOMP, **ENTONCES** no prometas simultáneamente una gran pérdida de grasa y una gran ganancia muscular; usa cintura + tendencia de peso + rendimiento + fotos como conjunto. citeturn14view4turn18search1 **2016/2015. Confianza: media.**

9. **SI** prescribes proteína a un adulto que hace fuerza, **ENTONCES** usa normalmente ~1.6–2.2 g/kg/día; en un déficit exigente puede ser razonable acercarse a ~1.8–2.4 g/kg/día. citeturn5search8turn14view4 **Morton et al., 2018; Longland et al., 2016. Confianza: alta/media-alta.**

10. **SI** el usuario ya cumple energía y proteína, **ENTONCES** no presentes el horario de nutrientes como el factor decisivo. Distribuye proteína en varias comidas y coloca una alrededor del entrenamiento por conveniencia, no por una “ventana” estrecha. citeturn6search18turn17search25 **2017. Confianza: media-alta.**

11. **SI** el entrenamiento es de volumen moderado y el usuario llega alimentado, **ENTONCES** no fuerces una ingesta de carbohidratos innecesariamente alta; **SI** el volumen es muy alto, hay dos sesiones/día o el rendimiento cae, **ENTONCES** prioriza más carbohidratos dentro de las calorías disponibles. citeturn19view5 **Henselmans et al., 2022. Confianza: media-alta.**

12. **SI** la persona pregunta por “el rango perfecto” de repeticiones, **ENTONCES** explica que hipertrofia funciona con cargas amplias; usa trabajo pesado para especificidad de fuerza y moderado/ligero para acumular hipertrofia eficientemente. citeturn15search14turn15search18 **2017/2021. Confianza: alta.**

13. **SI** el objetivo incluye hipertrofia, **ENTONCES** lleva la mayoría de series efectivas a aproximadamente 1–3 RIR; no exijas fallo en cada serie. citeturn3search24turn0search23 **2022/2024. Confianza: alta en “no hace falta fallo”; media-alta para 1–3 RIR.**

14. **SI** el usuario progresa con el volumen actual, **ENTONCES** no añadas series sólo porque “más es mejor”; el retorno del volumen disminuye conforme sube la dosis. citeturn14view0 **Pelland et al., 2026. Confianza: alta.**

15. **SI** un músculo no progresa durante varias semanas, técnica/esfuerzo son adecuados y recuperación es buena, **ENTONCES** prueba un pequeño aumento de volumen antes de rehacer todo el programa. **Heurística basada en la relación dosis-respuesta; confianza: media.** citeturn14view0

16. **SI** el volumen semanal es alto, **ENTONCES** distribúyelo en 2–3 exposiciones por músculo para mantener calidad; no afirmes que 3× es intrínsecamente superior a 2× para hipertrofia. citeturn14view0turn18search7 **2016/2026. Confianza: alta.**

17. **SI** un ejercicio produce dolor articular persistente o no permite progresar con buena técnica, **ENTONCES** cambia a una variante tolerable que entrene la misma función muscular; no existe obligación de usar una barra o ejercicio “sagrado”. El tipo de equipo no modifica consistentemente los resultados cuando el entrenamiento efectivo está igualado. citeturn19view0 **ACSM, 2026. Confianza: alta para flexibilidad de modalidad.**

18. **SI** hay caída de rendimiento durante varias sesiones más aumento de fatiga/soreness y baja motivación, **ENTONCES** reduce temporalmente volumen/esfuerzo antes de añadir entrenamiento. Un deload no necesita significar una semana completa sin levantar. citeturn15search3turn15search7 **2024. Confianza: media.**

19. **SI** el usuario afirma tener “grasa rebelde” localizada, **ENTONCES** verifica primero la tendencia de peso/cintura y adherencia; no prescribas abdominales, cardio en ayunas o protocolos regionales para quemar esa zona. citeturn14view6turn10search15 **2022/2017. Confianza: alta.**

20. **SI** el peso no cambia durante pocos días, **ENTONCES** no ajustes calorías. Usa una tendencia de varios días/semanas junto con cintura y adherencia. La duración exacta de observación es heurística. citeturn18search1turn13search23 **2015/2024. Confianza: media.**

21. **SI** el peso de un CUT no baja durante ~2–3 semanas y la adherencia es buena, **ENTONCES** realiza un solo cambio pequeño: menos calorías **o** algo más de actividad; después vuelve a observar. **Heurística de control; confianza: media-baja.**

22. **SI** la adherencia es claramente baja, **ENTONCES** no reduzcas automáticamente calorías teóricas; primero haz el plan más ejecutable. **Heurística conductual; confianza: media.**

23. **SI** el hambre de un CUT aumenta mucho pero el progreso sigue siendo adecuado, **ENTONCES** considera redistribución de comida o un diet break a mantenimiento por adherencia; no lo describas como “reactivar el metabolismo”. citeturn14view7 **Peos et al., 2021. Confianza: alta para ausencia de ventaja mágica; media para hambre/adherencia.**

24. **SI** el sueño se deteriora de forma persistente, **ENTONCES** evita simultáneamente subir volumen y hacer el déficit más agresivo. No extrapoles estudios agudos para inventar una pérdida porcentual de músculo. citeturn21view4turn21view5 **2020/2021. Confianza: media-alta.**

25. **SI** el usuario solicita fármacos, combinaciones estimulantes, hormonas, “stacks” o interpretación diagnóstica de laboratorios que no proporcionó un profesional, **ENTONCES** no prescribas, no inventes resultados y deriva a un humano cualificado. La farmacología descrita en literatura de adipose tissue es sólo contexto mecanístico. citeturn10search0 **Simonsen et al., 2008. Confianza: alta.**

**Weekly operating loop, para el agente**

Al cierre de cada semana: calcula tendencia de peso → revisa cintura/fotos cuando corresponda → compara rendimiento con las semanas previas → comprueba adherencia → revisa sueño, hambre, soreness/joints y actividad → decide **KEEP / ADJUST / DELOAD / DIET BREAK / ASK HUMAN**. Sólo una modificación importante por vez, salvo que exista un problema de seguridad. **Heurística de control; confianza: media.**

`KEEP` cuando la fase está produciendo la dirección esperada y la recuperación es aceptable. `ADJUST` cuando un trend suficientemente largo se desvía del objetivo con adherencia confirmada. `DELOAD` cuando la fatiga de entrenamiento, no la falta de estímulo, es la explicación más plausible. `DIET BREAK` cuando la restricción sigue siendo apropiada pero la adherencia/hambre necesita alivio. `ASK HUMAN` ante red flags médicos, nutricionales o psicológicos. Estas categorías son una síntesis operacional de la evidencia, no categorías clínicas. citeturn14view7turn15search3turn19view7 **Confianza: media.**

Estos datos permiten establecer el contexto pero no diagnostican estado de salud. **Confianza: alta como requisitos operativos.**

**Estado corporal**
- [ ] % de grasa estimado **y método**: visual, pliegues, BIA, DXA, otro.
- [ ] Cintura, con sitio de medición definido.
- [ ] Cadera opcional.
- [ ] Brazo/muslo/pecho opcionales según objetivo.
- [ ] Fotos estandarizadas disponibles: sí/no.
- [ ] Peso medio de los últimos 7–14 días, si existe.
- [ ] Cambio de peso durante las últimas 4–12 semanas.

El método y la estandarización son necesarios porque pequeñas diferencias de composición corporal pueden quedar dentro del error de medición. citeturn18search0turn18search1 **2015/2022. Confianza: alta.**

**Historial de entrenamiento**
- [ ] Años/meses de entrenamiento de fuerza **consistente**, no sólo tiempo desde la primera vez que fue al gimnasio.
- [ ] Frecuencia actual por semana.
- [ ] Programa actual y semanas utilizándolo.
- [ ] Ejercicios principales.
- [ ] Series duras aproximadas por músculo/semana.
- [ ] Rangos de repeticiones.
- [ ] RIR/RPE habitual, si sabe estimarlo.
- [ ] Cargas/repeticiones recientes en ejercicios comparables.
- [ ] ¿Los lifts están subiendo semanalmente, mensualmente o llevan meses estancados?
- [ ] Lesiones, dolor o ejercicios contraindicados por un profesional.
- [ ] Días y duración realistas disponibles para entrenar.

La progresión, volumen y carga son variables centrales; volumen muestra una relación dosis-respuesta con retornos decrecientes. citeturn14view0turn19view0 **Pelland et al., 2026; ACSM, 2026. Confianza: alta.**

**Nutrición actual**
- [ ] Calorías medias actuales, si se registran.
- [ ] Proteína diaria aproximada.
- [ ] Número y horario general de comidas.
- [ ] Preferencias/restricciones alimentarias.
- [ ] Hambre actual: baja / manejable / alta.
- [ ] Historial reciente de CUT/maintenance/gain.
- [ ] Mayor pérdida de peso reciente y ritmo aproximado.
- [ ] Episodios de atracón/restricción o relación problemática con tracking: sí/no.
- [ ] Capacidad/preferencia para pesar comida: sí/no.
- [ ] Número de comidas fuera de casa por semana.

Energía y proteína son prioridades más robustas que timing fino; antecedentes de conducta alimentaria problemática son un gate de seguridad, no una oportunidad para imponer tracking más rígido. citeturn5search8turn18search10 **Morton et al., 2018; IOC subgroup review, 2023. Confianza: alta.**

**Actividad y cardio**
- [ ] Pasos diarios promedio o nivel de actividad habitual.
- [ ] Trabajo sedentario / mixto / físicamente activo.
- [ ] Cardio actual: modalidad, frecuencia, duración e intensidad.
- [ ] Deporte adicional.
- [ ] Cambios recientes de actividad.
- [ ] Preferencia entre caminar, bicicleta, correr, intervalos u otras modalidades.

El cardio puede coexistir con fuerza, aunque una carga concurrente excesiva puede generar pequeñas interferencias específicas; por eso debe contabilizarse como carga total. citeturn15search2turn15search17 **Schumann et al., 2022; Huiberts et al., 2024. Confianza: alta/media.**

**Recuperación**
- [ ] Horas habituales de sueño y variabilidad.
- [ ] Calidad subjetiva de sueño.
- [ ] Trabajo nocturno/turnos.
- [ ] Estrés de vida: bajo / medio / alto.
- [ ] Soreness persistente.
- [ ] Dolor articular.
- [ ] Motivación para entrenar.
- [ ] Fatiga inusual.
- [ ] Rendimiento reciente: subiendo / estable / bajando.
- [ ] Días reales disponibles para recuperación.

La restricción severa de sueño puede reducir agudamente la síntesis proteica muscular, aunque no existe una fórmula que convierta horas perdidas de sueño en porcentaje de hipertrofia perdida. citeturn21view4turn21view5 **Saner et al., 2020; Lamon et al., 2021. Confianza: alta para el efecto agudo, baja para cuantificar efecto crónico.**

**Safety / ASK HUMAN gate**
- [ ] Embarazo o lactancia.
- [ ] Trastorno de alimentación actual/pasado o sospecha relevante.
- [ ] Amenorrea/cambio menstrual importante no explicado.
- [ ] Fracturas por estrés/recurrentes.
- [ ] Mareos, desmayos o síntomas cardiovasculares con ejercicio.
- [ ] Enfermedad crónica que pueda afectar entrenamiento/nutrición.
- [ ] Cirugía o lesión reciente.
- [ ] Medicamentos que puedan afectar apetito, peso, frecuencia cardiaca, glucosa, presión arterial o rendimiento.
- [ ] Síntomas médicos no diagnosticados.
- [ ] Solicitud de hormonas, fármacos, “fat burners” o stacks.
- [ ] Laboratorios clínicos: **sólo registrar valores realmente proporcionados; nunca inventarlos, solicitar paneles diagnósticos como si el agente fuera médico ni interpretar anomalías fuera del alcance del coach.**

Problemas de baja disponibilidad energética/RED-S requieren evaluación clínica cuando hay signos relevantes; un agente de recomposición debe detectar el gate, no realizar el diagnóstico. citeturn19view7 **IOC, 2023. Confianza: alta.**

**Output obligatorio del Frame Assessment**
- [ ] Ruta seleccionada: **RECOMP / CUT / LEAN GAIN / ASK HUMAN**.

- [ ] Nivel de confianza.
- [ ] Objetivo de tendencia de peso.
- [ ] Proteína inicial.
- [ ] Calorías iniciales o decisión explícita de observar mantenimiento antes de fijarlas.
- [ ] Volumen inicial por músculo como **starting volume**, nunca presentado como “MRV científico”.
- [ ] Frecuencia de entrenamiento.
- [ ] RIR objetivo.
- [ ] Cardio/actividad de referencia.
- [ ] KPIs que se revisarán semanalmente.
- [ ] Criterios escritos de KEEP / ADJUST / DELOAD / DIET BREAK / ASK HUMAN.
- [ ] Próxima decisión basada en **datos observados**, no en una estimación inventada.

Esta última salida convierte los tres marcos canónicos en un sistema cerrado y conservador: **entrena progresivamente; controla energía; cubre proteína; distribuye la carga de modo recuperable; mide tendencias; cambia poco y sólo cuando los datos lo justifican; trata “stubborn fat” como fisiología regional, no como permiso para spot-reduction hacks; y detén la automatización en la frontera médica.** Ese núcleo es el que cuenta con el apoyo más consistente de la evidencia contemporánea. citeturn19view0turn14view0turn5search8turn14view6turn19view7 **ACSM/Pelland/Morton/Ramirez-Campillo/IOC, 2018–2026.
